# Postwar national environment rebuild

The national House vote estimate now comes from a fitted historical model. It uses [Brookings Vital Statistics table 2-2](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/vital-statistics-on-congress/) for Democratic and Republican House vote shares in every election from 1946 through 2024, and dated [American Presidency Project approval observations](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/statistics/data/presidential-job-approval-all-data). The files and SHA-256 manifest are frozen in `data/reference/national_environment/`; `python modeling/national_environment.py --download` refreshes them. The current approval input is the NYT approval average already stored in Stage 1.

For each election from 1948 through 2024, the script reconstructs what was known 43 days before Election Day. It selects approval observations whose fieldwork ended by that date, averages those in the 45 days preceding the latest eligible observation, and records how old that observation was. The outcome is the national Democratic two party House vote margin. Predictors are the previous House vote margin, the sitting president's party, whether the race is a midterm, and the party signed presidential net approval. The model has 39 election cycles and 1,918 usable noninterpolated approval observations. The earliest cycles sometimes have approval observations several months old; those ages are retained for audit.

A ridge regression fits the coefficients. Candidate regularization strengths and exponential recency half lives are compared using rolling predictions trained only on earlier elections through 2016. Elections in 2018, 2020, 2022, and 2024 are reserved for a later check. The selected 24-year half life and 0.001 ridge penalty produce a 5.27-point rolling tuning RMSE and a 2.95-point RMSE in those four later elections. Individual later errors are +1.17, +4.88, -2.47, and +1.88 points (prediction minus outcome). Four elections cannot establish precise calibration; the 2020 error illustrates the remaining risk.

An approval ablation with the same recency and regularization settings gives 5.36-point rolling tuning RMSE and 2.28-point later-cycle RMSE. Thus approval offers a small historical tuning gain but no demonstrated gain in the four recent checks. It moves the current fitted estimate from D+6.31 without approval to D+8.73 with approval. The coefficient is fitted, but its predictive value needs more scrutiny; no separate economy or war coefficient has been added without a comparably dated historical series.

Using the 2024 national House vote (R+2.58) and the September 21, 2026 NYT presidential average (37.7% approve, 59.4% disapprove), the fitted historical estimate is **D+8.73** in the national two party House vote, with a 5.27-point standard error based on the rolling historical errors. This near match to Silver's separately published D+8.7 benchmark was not used for fitting or tuning. The estimates update if the approval snapshot changes.

Stage 4 now replaces the aggregate national center of its race-results draws with this estimate before adding current generic ballot polls. The original race draws still provide candidate allocation and correlated race residuals; the Senate local mean has since been fitted separately. The original local generic-ballot archive has only one well-populated cycle (2020). A newly frozen FiveThirtyEight reconstructed series provides 11 older cycles, but its comparability with the current NYT feed has not been established. The current combination treats their errors as independent and remains an **internal forecast**; the parameter artifact shows the separate fundamentals estimate and error-width sensitivities. No Senate-specific interval multiplier was introduced.

The national model predicts House popular vote, not Senate or gubernatorial popular vote. Applying its shift to those races assumes common national partisan movement, while local polls can override it. Evaluating that transfer and replacing the thin generic-ballot error estimate are required before publication.

## Local lean and the remaining Senate gap

The current race baseline starts from the previous result in the same seat. The shared national shift centers those race draws, so their deviations from the old aggregate are used as local lean. This is a useful first approximation, but a previous Senate result mixes state partisanship with that year's national environment, the previous candidates, and incumbency. It cannot by itself identify a stable state lean. No Senate-only interval widening was added; that would change confidence without correcting the race means.

**Update:** The local-baseline fit changed conditional Democratic Senate control from 41.9% to 39.5% in its original run; see [the local-lean rebuild](senate_local_lean_rebuild.md). A later [multi-cycle Senate poll correction](senate_poll_bias_recalibration.md) changed that result to 37.6%; the [prior-winner name-match repair](senate_race_trace_2026.md) brought the current result to 38.1%. The national model and generic-ballot combination described above are unchanged.

**Later update (version 0.5):** The approval fit remains available as a structural diagnostic, but the live nowcast no longer combines it independently with the well-polled generic ballot. See [the generic-first update](generic_first_nowcast_2026.md) for the current rule and numbers. The older figures above document prior model versions.
